You should buy a used Thinkpad.

<!-- more -->

Seriously. Go on Ebay right now and search for a Thinkpad. No matter who you
are, I promise it will be worth it. The durability and longevity of Tinkpads
means you can spend as little as $70 and have a solid machine that will further
last you years.

## Why?

You might be wondering why you should bother even considering purchasing an old
Thinkpad in the first place. After all, you probably own another laptop, and at
this price point all of the functioning laptops are likely almost a decade old.
Although this may be true, there are also a few distinct advantages these
laptops have over even the top-of-the-line laptops of today.

### Price

The first and probably most compelling reason to buy a used Thinkpad is their
price. With some good searching, you can find one in reasonable condition for
under $100. They might be missing nonessential components, like an external
battery, but otherwise boot and run perfectly fine. The value of these laptops
increase even further when compared with new laptops at this price, which are
usually worse in quality and performance for a higher price.

Besides just being great deals, having a cheap laptop allows you to do things
you would never even consider doing with a newer, more expensive laptop. You can
carry it around without worrying about dropping it or try modding the hardware.
If it breaks, who cares? At the end of the day, you spent less than $100 on it,
and you can move on with your life.

### Quality

The Thinkpads in this range are built like tanks. They have some of the best
build quality I have felt on a laptop. You can push them quite far physically
without worrying about them breaking, which, when combined with my previous
point about their price means that you have a laptop that will likely never
break, but even if it does, you won't be losing much.

They are built so well, in fact, that I've been using my T470 almost half the
time, choosing to use it over even my much newer and much more powerful laptop.
There's just something about these machines that makes them so satisfying to
use. Their keyboards are amazing to type on, and the trackpoint and mouse
buttons integrate very well into my keyboard-centric workflow.

### Upgradability/repairability

Lastly, you should buy a Thinkpad, especially an old one, because they are very
easy to repair and upgrade. This is nice for a couple reasons. First, it means
that if your Thinkpad does actually break, you will likely be able to repair it,
and for a reasonable price. For under $50, you can get a duplicate of your
Thinkpad without any memory or storage, and then you can just mix-and-match
until you have one working machine. Again, this adds to the narrative that you
literally don't need to worry about your Thinkpad. It likely won't break at all,
but if it does, you can either cut your (low) losses, or repair it for very
cheap.

Additionally, you can upgrade your used Thinkpad with minimal effort. You can
install a massive battery, upgrade the RAM if you are feeling rich, or upgrade
your keyboard to a backlit one. Each of there upgrades can be performed in less
than five minutes if you know what you're doing, and even if you don't, you will
learn a lot about computer hardware in the process.

## What?

Now that you have a new old Thinkpad, what should you even do with it?
Obviously, at its core it is just a normal laptop, so you can do normal laptop
things on it. But that's boring, so here's a few more things you can do.

### Install Linux

Old Thinkpads are the perfect machines to install Linux on. Their age means that
they will be supported on even the most stable of distros, and many of them are
Linux certified, so you won't run into any compatibility issues. Additionally,
Linux requires fewer system resources than Windows, so you will likely get more
out of your limited processor and memory.

If you bought the Thinkpad to learn more about computers by upgrading and
repairing it, then Linux is the perfect next step to take. Linux will teach you
a lot about how computers work under the hood and will likely give you a new
perspective on computing as a whole. Because this is probably not your main
machine, you have much more leeway to experiment with the operating system, and
you can “disto hop” without worrying about messing up your workflow, as you can
always return to your main machine.

### Durability test

If you bought your Thinkpad as a “throwaway” machine, then you might as well do
some durability tests on it. Drop it from a large height, hit it with a hammer,
or try to bend it in half with your bare hands. If you break it, then the
modularity of old Thinkpads makes it very easy to repurpose all of the parts in
your broken laptop. There exist so many guides online about how to repurpose the
speakers, screen (even if broken), keyboard, webcam, and more. You can also sell
your RAM and SSD for their weight in gold and maybe even make a profit.

### Repair it

Most Thinkpads you will find at this price will have something broken on them.
They might have missing keys, a cracked screen, or a scratched chassis. The
nature of Thinkpads means you can repair these cosmetic damages very easily.
Replacing a keyboard takes a few minutes, and most other things can be replaced
in less than half an hour.

## My Thinkpad

Of course, I do own a Thinkpad, and I did buy it for $70 plus shipping. It came
with a 6th generation i5 processor, 16 GB of RAM, and a 256 GB SSD. All things
considered, these are perfectly fine specs, especially for a secondary Linux
laptop. I initially installed Fedora on it just to try it out, but I will soon
switch it to NixOS to make it more in line with the rest of my machines.

I had to purchase an external battery and charger, although I bought them from
third parties for a combined total of and additional $30. As an aside, I'm not
sure how harmful it is to use non-OEM parts, but I haven't had any issues so
far. Another cool thing about specifically old Thinkpads is the hot-swappable
external battery, which also comes in a few different sizes. I bought the
largest 72 Watt-hour battery, bringing the total battery up to a massive 96
Watt-hours, which gives me multiple days of use out of the low-powered
processor. However, the larger battery is also physically larger, giving the
Thinkpad a large raised area on the back that makes it more difficult to fit in
a backpack. There is also a smaller, 24 Watt-hour external battery, which sits
flush with the rest of the chassis at the cost of less battery life.

### Disaster

Last week, I brought my Thinkpad on a trip with me, and something terrible
happened: my water bottle leaked on the plane, and it soaked the laptop. That
night, I decided to be impatient and tried turning on the laptop. Of course, it
hadn't fully dried by this point and the laptop wouldn't turn on. I did not
despair, however, for the reasons listed above. I knew that even if I threw the
laptop away at that point, I wouldnt've lost more than $100.

I did not throw it away, however. I simply ordered another Thinkpad of the same
model with a known good motherboard for $40. This one did not come with RAM or
storage, but I was confident the RAM and SSD were not damaged by the water. The
donor laptop has not arrived yet, but when it does, I will write an update on
how the replacement goes.
